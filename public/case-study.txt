DESIGNING LONGITUDINAL WEIGHT-LOSS CARE AT AMAZON ONE MEDICAL

How I helped turn a one-off telehealth product into a cash-pay, longitudinal GLP-1 care experience

Amazon Health Services / Amazon One Medical
Role: Sole Product Designer
Duration: ~1 year end-to-end, with ~6–7 months of core product design
Scope: Cash-pay GLP-1 weight-loss care within Pay-per-visit / On-Demand Care
Themes: 0→1 product design, systems thinking, healthcare, growth, cross-functional execution


OVERVIEW

Amazon One Medical historically served two different kinds of healthcare needs.

One was ongoing primary care, where patients build a longer-term relationship with One Medical and may use insurance.

The other was Pay-per-visit, or PPV: a simpler cash-pay telehealth product designed for common, one-off conditions such as pink eye, acne, sinus infections, or UTIs. A customer chooses a condition, completes an intake, sees a provider, receives a treatment plan, and is usually done.

My project sat inside the second model.

The business wanted to expand PPV beyond episodic care and test whether it could support a more longitudinal healthcare use case. Weight loss was a natural place to start because GLP-1 medications had become a major area of demand, but the treatment model is fundamentally different from treating an acute condition.

A GLP-1 weight-loss patient may need to be screened for eligibility, complete lab work, meet a clinician, receive and adjust medication over time, fill prescriptions through a pharmacy, manage side effects, return for follow-up visits, renew treatment, and receive reminders over many months.

That meant the product could no longer behave like a single transaction.

The core design challenge became:

How do we make a clinically responsible, cash-pay weight-loss program feel simple and coherent when the underlying care journey spans many months, multiple visits, multiple systems, and multiple partner teams?

As the sole product designer for the cash-pay GLP-1 experience, I worked across the end-to-end journey and the system underneath it: discovery, qualification, clinical intake, labs, medication preference, pharmacy choice, video visit, treatment plan, follow-up care, reminders, email, notifications, and the longitudinal patient-state model connecting those moments.

The most important product decision I drove was the introduction of a persistent “Case” above individual visits. Instead of treating each visit as an unrelated transaction, the Case connected the patient’s labs, treatment plan, prescriptions, messaging, follow-ups, and renewals into one ongoing treatment journey.

Before launch, I tested the complete experience with six participants. All six understood the overall program structure, validating the longitudinal model. The study also surfaced two high-severity conversion and trust gaps: customers did not understand the total cost of treatment over time, and medication selection was too heavily framed around price instead of clinical differences.

Those findings changed where we focused the final design work before launch.


1. THE BUSINESS AND PRODUCT CONTEXT

WHAT AMAZON ONE MEDICAL ALREADY HAD

Before this work, PPV was optimized for episodic telehealth.

The typical mental model was:

Condition → Intake → Visit → Treatment plan → Short follow-up window → Done

That model works well for something like pink eye. A customer has a problem now, wants a fast answer, a provider evaluates them, and the condition usually resolves. The product does not need to maintain a complicated long-term treatment state.

WHAT WE WANTED TO ADD

The company wanted to expand into longitudinal care while preserving the simplicity of the cash-pay PPV model.

Weight loss created that opportunity.

This was not an “uninsured-only” product. The target was broader: customers willing to pay directly for weight-loss care rather than rely on an insurance-based pathway. Some may not have coverage for GLP-1 treatment. Some may have coverage but not want to navigate prior authorization or a longer primary-care process. Others may value the convenience and predictability of a direct-pay virtual service.

The important business distinction was:

Traditional One Medical care could be insurance-based.
This product was specifically the cash-pay pathway.

That distinction affected almost every design decision: pricing transparency, visit structure, medication costs, pharmacy handoffs, and the need to make the entire journey understandable before a customer committed to treatment.


2. WHY GLP-1 CARE BROKE THE EXISTING PPV MODEL

At first glance, it might sound simple to add “Weight Loss” as another condition in PPV.

But GLP-1 care breaks several assumptions that episodic telehealth is built around.

Episodic care:
Condition → Visit → Treatment plan → Done

Longitudinal weight-loss care:
Eligibility → Clinical intake → Labs → First visit → Medication decision → Pharmacy fulfillment → Monitoring → Follow-up → Dose changes → Renewal → Repeat

There were five important differences.

FIRST: THE UNIT OF CARE WAS NO LONGER A SINGLE VISIT

A second weight-loss visit depends on the first.

The system needs to know:
What medication was prescribed?
Did the patient complete their labs?
What dose are they currently taking?
Are they tolerating treatment?
Is the next visit a follow-up or a new start?
Is a renewal due?
Is messaging still open?
Is the patient active, paused, or no longer in treatment?

This meant we needed a product model that could remember and organize patient state over time.

SECOND: ELIGIBILITY CHANGED THE JOURNEY ITSELF

GLP-1 treatment has real contraindications and clinical requirements.

A patient’s state, BMI, medical history, medications, pregnancy status, prior GLP-1 use, and lab availability can all change what happens next.

Some patients can continue normally.
Some can continue only after an additional action or warning.
Some cannot safely or legally receive treatment through this pathway.

Clinical and regulatory logic therefore directly shaped the interaction design.

THIRD: IMPORTANT PARTS OF CARE HAPPENED OUTSIDE ONE MEDICAL

Patients might need lab work from an external lab partner.
They might fill a prescription through Amazon Pharmacy or another pharmacy.

The customer could move between Amazon, One Medical, Labcorp, and Pharmacy surfaces during one treatment journey.

The product therefore had to feel coherent across multiple teams and systems.

FOURTH: MEDICATION CHANGES OVER TIME

Treatment may begin at a starter dose and evolve as the patient progresses.

That makes the treatment plan dynamic rather than one-and-done.

FIFTH: CUSTOMERS HAD TO RETURN

In episodic care, the product can wait for the customer to come back.

In longitudinal care, the system has to bring the customer back at the right moment.

That made reminders, emails, notifications, treatment-plan availability, follow-up prompts, and renewal communication part of the product itself rather than secondary marketing channels.


3. MY ROLE AND OWNERSHIP

I was the sole product designer for the cash-pay GLP-1 experience.

My work covered the end-to-end customer journey as well as the underlying product logic that connected the experience over time.

I partnered primarily with product and engineering, while coordinating with:

One Medical clinical stakeholders
Legal and regulatory partners
Amazon Pharmacy PM and design
Lab partners, including Labcorp
Amazon and One Medical discovery surfaces
Adjacent One Medical platform teams
Lifecycle communication surfaces such as email and notifications

A large part of the job was translating clinical, legal, operational, and platform requirements into understandable product behavior.

Clinical stakeholders defined the care rules.
Legal and regulatory partners helped define what was allowed and what restrictions or disclosures were required.

My role was to determine how a patient experienced those rules:

When should we ask a question?
When should we stop someone?
When should we warn them?
What should we explain?
What can they do next?
Can they recover and continue later?
How do we avoid making necessary clinical friction feel like a random product error?

I was not deciding who medically qualified for GLP-1 treatment.

I was designing how complex eligibility and care rules became a clear, trustworthy customer experience.


4. STARTING WITH THE LONGITUDINAL SYSTEM, NOT INDIVIDUAL SCREENS

A normal screen-by-screen flow was not enough to understand this product.

I mapped the treatment experience across roughly 15 months.

The longitudinal model included several tracks running in parallel:

Medication usage
PPV visits
Medication delivery
Reminder notifications
Messaging windows
Lab requirements
Renewals and follow-ups

That exercise exposed dependencies that were easy to miss if we looked at only one visit at a time.

For example:

A patient might complete a first visit now but need labs before continuing later.

Medication could be delivered monthly while follow-up visits happened at a different cadence.

A messaging window might open after a visit and expire before the next one.

A patient returning three months later should not restart from the beginning.

A lab requirement might be introduced early but affect what a clinician can prescribe later.

The longitudinal map became a shared model for the whole team.

It helped us think about patient state rather than just screen sequence.

That became the foundation for the most important product architecture decision in the project.


5. THE KEY PRODUCT DECISION: INTRODUCING THE “CASE”

The existing PPV model centered around individual visits.

That was a problem for longitudinal care.

If every visit remained independent, a patient’s weight-loss treatment would appear as a series of unrelated transactions.

I considered three broad approaches.

OPTION 1: KEEP EVERY VISIT INDEPENDENT

Advantage:
This was the easiest option technically because it reused the existing PPV structure.

Tradeoff:
The patient would not have a clear place to understand their overall treatment. Labs, prescriptions, messages, and follow-up care would feel fragmented, and the product would have no clear home for longitudinal state.

OPTION 2: BUILD AN ENTIRELY NEW SUBSCRIPTION OR PROGRAM PRODUCT

Advantage:
This model would naturally support long-term care.

Tradeoff:
It would require a much larger business and engineering change. It would move away from the simplicity of PPV and potentially require a different pricing model and new infrastructure.

OPTION 3: INTRODUCE A CASE ABOVE EXISTING VISITS

This became the model I proposed.

The individual visit would continue to exist.

But a persistent Weight Loss Case would sit above those visits and connect the broader treatment journey.

A Case could contain:

Initial visit
Labs
Treatment plan
Prescription
Pharmacy fulfillment
Messaging
Follow-up visits
Renewals
Ongoing patient state

This gave us continuity without requiring us to rebuild the entire PPV platform.

The simplest way to explain the decision is:

The visit remained a transaction.
The Case became the relationship.

It let the product support longitudinal care while still using many of the underlying primitives that already existed.


6. DESIGNING THE ELIGIBILITY SYSTEM

The customer-facing clinical intake looked like a questionnaire.

Underneath it was a much more complex decision system.

The flow had to account for factors such as:

State-level restrictions
Weight-loss goals
Prior bariatric surgery
Current or previous GLP-1 use
BMI and related conditions
Diabetes history
Kidney and gallbladder conditions
Mental-health considerations
Current medications
Pregnancy or nursing
Lab availability

Across the experience, there were roughly 18 hard-stop conditions and 8 soft-stop or warning conditions.

The exact medical rules came from clinical, legal, and regulatory requirements.

My design responsibility was to make those rules usable.

I grouped outcomes into three broad interaction patterns.

CONTINUE
The patient can proceed normally.

CONTINUE WITH GUIDANCE
The patient may still be able to proceed, but they need to understand a risk, complete an action, provide evidence, or acknowledge something first.

STOP
The service cannot safely or legally treat the patient through this pathway.

The hardest design question was not simply how the stop state looked.

It was what the patient should understand and do next.

For every important stop state, I tried to answer four questions:

What happened?
Why does it matter?
Can I fix it?
What should I do next?

For example, if the customer lacked acceptable lab results, the system needed to tell them what was missing, what counted as acceptable evidence, where they could get the required labs, and whether they could return later.

This led to one of the design principles I used throughout the project:

Clinical friction should feel purposeful, not arbitrary.

In a growth product, reducing friction is usually desirable.

In healthcare, some friction is necessary.

The design challenge is making sure the customer understands why it exists.


7. THE END-TO-END CUSTOMER JOURNEY

The final experience covered the full treatment journey rather than a single clinical encounter.

DISCOVERY

Customers could discover the service through several front doors, including general search, Amazon surfaces, Amazon Pharmacy, and One Medical.

Someone arriving from a weight-loss treatment page might be choosing a care service.

Someone arriving from a Wegovy or Zepbound-oriented medication surface might already be thinking about a specific drug and simply need a prescription.

The product therefore had to orient customers quickly and explain the role of One Medical.

PROGRAM UNDERSTANDING

Before starting, customers needed to understand:

What the program was
What the first visit cost
That medication cost was separate
That labs might be required
That a prescription was not guaranteed
That ongoing treatment would involve follow-up care
That the experience was cash-pay

CLINICAL INTAKE

Customers entered health history, weight and height, goals, previous GLP-1 experience, medications, allergies, and other relevant health information.

The intake simultaneously collected information for the clinician and routed the customer through the eligibility logic.

LABS

Some customers already had valid lab results.

Others needed new testing.

The product had to explain which labs were required, what counted, and what happened if the customer did not have them.

MEDICATION PREFERENCE

Customers could express a preferred medication or choose to let the clinician decide.

This was intentionally a preference, not a shopping selection.

The clinician remained responsible for determining whether a medication was appropriate.

PHARMACY

Customers could use Amazon Pharmacy or another pharmacy.

Amazon Pharmacy was a convenient fulfillment path, but it was not mandatory.

VISIT

The first GLP-1 visit was a higher-touch clinical moment than many episodic PPV conditions.

The provider reviewed the patient’s history, labs, goals, and medication suitability.

TREATMENT PLAN

After the visit, the patient received a treatment plan connecting the clinical decision to what happened next.

It could include:

Visit summary
Provider guidance
Prescribed medication
Supply and refill details
Messaging availability
Injection education or related resources
A direct path to Amazon Pharmacy when appropriate

FOLLOW-UP CARE

The journey did not end with the treatment plan.

The Case connected the patient to future visits, renewals, labs, and ongoing treatment state.


8. CROSS-TEAM WORK: LABCORP

One of the clearest examples of systems complexity was the lab experience.

The medical requirement originated inside One Medical.

But the customer might have to leave One Medical and complete testing through an external lab partner.

We worked with Labcorp as one of those pathways.

Amazon customers received special discounted pricing through the partnership.

The flow crossed organizational boundaries:

One Medical tells the customer they need labs
→ customer enters a Labcorp flow
→ purchases or schedules testing
→ completes the test
→ results become part of the care journey
→ customer returns to care

Our responsibility did not stop at the link.

Because customers experienced it as one Amazon-supported journey, we reviewed and approved the Labcorp-facing flow.

The customer needed to understand:

What tests were required
Why they were required
Where they could get them
What the discounted price covered
What happened after testing
How they could return to care

This later showed up directly in usability testing.

Participants generally understood why labs were needed.

The bigger problem was operational clarity: where to get them and how much they would cost.

That reinforced a broader idea:

When a critical customer action leaves your product, the handoff itself becomes part of your UX.


9. CROSS-TEAM WORK: AMAZON PHARMACY

I also worked closely with the Amazon Pharmacy PM and designer.

This was another cross-org journey where customers could move in both directions.

A customer might begin on a medication-oriented Amazon Pharmacy surface.

If they already had a prescription, Pharmacy could help fulfill it.

If they needed a prescription, the experience could route them into One Medical care.

After seeing a clinician, the customer could return to Amazon Pharmacy to fulfill the prescription.

The loop looked roughly like:

Medication discovery
→ Need a prescription
→ One Medical intake and visit
→ Clinician treatment decision
→ Treatment plan
→ Amazon Pharmacy fulfillment

The challenge was that this superficially resembles e-commerce.

But it could not behave like a normal shopping flow.

We had to preserve several distinctions:

Choosing a medication preference did not guarantee that medication.
The customer was paying separately for clinical care and medication.
A clinician, not a shopping interface, determined appropriate treatment.
Amazon Pharmacy was a fulfillment option, not a requirement.
Medication availability and pricing could vary.

This became especially relevant during usability testing because our first version made medication comparison too price-led.

The design had to balance transparent cash-pay pricing with the responsibility of making a medical decision feel clinically informed rather than transactional.


10. USER RESEARCH: TESTING THE COMPLETE V1

Once the first end-to-end experience was coherent, I ran an unmoderated usability study through UserTesting.

We tested an unbranded Figma prototype with six participants.

The sample included both people new to GLP-1 medications and people with previous GLP-1 experience.

We tested the complete journey rather than isolated screens.

The study focused on four questions.

PROGRAM UNDERSTANDING AND COST

Did customers understand the scope of the program, how much it could cost, and how much time or follow-up it might require?

LABS

Did customers understand what labs were required, why they were required, and how to provide or obtain them?

MEDICATION PREFERENCE

Could customers compare medication options and understand pharmacy choices without confusion?

TREATMENT PLAN

After the first visit, did customers understand dosage, next steps, and how to access ongoing care?

The most important overall result was positive.

All 6 of 6 participants found the overall program structure understandable.

Participants rated the flow from “semi-easy” to “very easy.”

The research recommendation was not to restructure the experience.

That mattered because it validated the larger systems model.

The longitudinal architecture itself was working.

The problems were concentrated in a few moments where trust and conversion were especially fragile.


11. FINDING 1: CUSTOMERS DID NOT THINK ABOUT PRICE TRANSACTIONALLY

Severity: High
Frequency: 5 of 6 participants, or 83%

This was the most common usability problem.

Pricing information existed throughout the product.

The first visit had a price.
Medication had a price.
Labs could have a price.
Future visits would also cost money.

We had initially treated these as separate pieces of information.

But customers were not evaluating them separately.

They were trying to understand:

“How much is this whole treatment going to cost me over time?”

Participants asked questions such as:

Does the medication become more expensive as my dose increases?
How much will future visits cost?
How long will I need to stay on treatment?
What do labs cost?
What is my total financial commitment?

Two participants said cost could directly make them unlikely to use the program.

One said that it appeared cheaper elsewhere.

That changed the design problem.

The problem was no longer:

Show each price clearly.

It became:

Help the customer understand the financial commitment of longitudinal treatment.

We moved toward consolidating pricing information and making the relationship between visit fees, medication, labs, and ongoing care more explicit.

For this cash-pay product, price transparency was not just informational content.

It was a trust feature and a conversion feature.


12. FINDING 2: MEDICATION SELECTION FELT TOO MUCH LIKE SHOPPING

Severity: High
Frequency: 4 of 6 participants, or 67%

Our early medication experience made price easy to compare.

That seemed logical for a cash-pay service.

But testing revealed an unintended effect.

Participants wanted to understand more than price.

They looked for:

Dosing frequency
Administration method
Common side effects
Expected efficacy
Clinical differences between medications

One participant described the experience as feeling like it was “all wrapped around cost.”

Another said they would not know which option was best because the experience did not explain the differences clearly enough.

We had tried to make the experience transparent.

But by making price the easiest differentiator, we risked turning a medical decision into a shopping decision.

I rebalanced the medication experience so customers had more clinical context alongside price.

At the same time, we continued to make clear that the customer was expressing a preference, not choosing a guaranteed prescription.

The clinician remained responsible for the final treatment decision.

This captured one of the central tensions in the project:

Make access easy enough to convert, without making treatment feel casually transactional.


13. FINDING 3: LABS WERE UNDERSTOOD, BUT OBTAINING THEM WAS NOT ALWAYS CLEAR

Severity: Medium
Frequency: 2 of 6 participants, or 33%

Participants generally understood that labs were required.

The problem was not the medical rationale.

The problem was the handoff.

Customers wanted clearer answers to questions such as:

Where can I actually get these labs?
Are there alternatives to the partner lab?
How much will it cost?
Why am I only seeing the full price after I click into the partner flow?

We responded by making lab options and costs more prominent.

This reinforced a broader systems-design lesson:

When a critical customer action leaves your product, the handoff itself becomes part of your UX.


14. WHAT THE USABILITY TEST VALIDATED

The study did not tell us that the product needed a new structure.

It told us the opposite.

6 of 6 participants understood the overall flow.

The journey from intake to treatment plan was logical and well received.

That meant the core longitudinal architecture was sound.

The issues were concentrated in three places:

Total cost transparency
Medication decision confidence
External lab procurement

Instead of redesigning the entire product, we could focus effort on the moments most likely to damage trust or conversion.


15. DESIGNING BEYOND THE CORE APP: EMAILS AND NOTIFICATIONS

Longitudinal care does not work if the product only communicates when the patient opens the app or website.

Patients have actions that become important at different times.

They may need to complete labs.
A treatment plan may be ready.
A follow-up may be due.
A renewal may be approaching.
A messaging window may be closing.

I also designed lifecycle communication across notifications and email.

These were not side marketing assets.

They were part of the care model.

The purpose was to bring the next relevant action back to the customer at the right time.

That also made the work relevant to growth.

In a longitudinal service, activation is not only “did the user complete the first visit?”

The business also depends on customers successfully continuing through labs, prescriptions, follow-ups, and renewals.

Email and notifications therefore supported both continuity of care and conversion across the lifecycle.


16. AMAZON-SCALE COMPLEXITY

The difficult part of “Amazon scale” was not simply that Amazon has many users.

The complexity came from the number of systems and teams that had to behave like one coherent customer experience.

The journey could touch:

Amazon Search or other discovery surfaces
One Medical
The PPV platform
Clinical policy
Legal and regulatory review
Labcorp and other lab pathways
Amazon Pharmacy
Email and notifications
Adjacent One Medical product teams

Every team had its own systems, constraints, timelines, and ownership boundaries.

The customer saw none of that.

The design goal was to make the experience feel like one service.

A large part of my work involved aligning across product boundaries, reviewing adjacent experiences, making state transitions clear, and designing for what happened when the customer moved from one system to another.

This is why the project is a systems-thinking case study rather than simply a healthcare UI case study.


17. LAUNCH

The product moved through beta testing and a staged production rollout rather than going directly to full traffic.

That approach was especially important in healthcare because errors have a different risk profile than a typical consumer product.

A confusing e-commerce flow can reduce conversion.

A confusing healthcare flow can also affect patient trust, clinical safety, legal compliance, or whether someone understands what action they should take next.

The staged launch allowed the team to watch the experience under real use before widening exposure.

The exact production metrics should be added here once available.

Useful metrics would include:

Completed new visits
Intake completion
Eligible-to-paid conversion
Lab completion
Amazon Pharmacy attachment
Follow-up visit rate
Renewal rate
Drop-off by step
Customer support or confusion signals

Do not invent these metrics.

If exact data is unavailable, the accurate claim is:

The experience launched through beta and a staged production rollout, and the pre-launch usability study validated the core architecture while identifying the highest-priority trust and conversion gaps.


18. THE RESULT

The project expanded PPV from a product designed around one-off transactions into a model that could support a longitudinal cash-pay treatment journey.

The finished system connected:

Discovery
Clinical qualification
Intake
Labs
Medication preference
Video visit
Treatment plan
Pharmacy fulfillment
Messaging
Follow-up care
Renewals
Lifecycle communication

The most important product result was not a single screen.

It was a new way for the product to represent an ongoing relationship.

The Case created continuity above individual visits.

The eligibility system translated clinical and regulatory complexity into patient-facing states.

Labcorp and Amazon Pharmacy integrations made external handoffs part of one customer journey.

Email and notification work extended the product beyond the moment of the visit.

The usability study validated the overall architecture:

6 of 6 participants understood the program structure.
5 of 6 exposed a major total-cost transparency gap.
4 of 6 showed that medication selection needed more clinical context.
2 of 6 identified friction around external lab procurement.

The research allowed us to preserve the core model while focusing iteration on the moments where trust and conversion were most fragile.


19. HOW HEALTH AI CHANGED THE LONGER-TERM PRODUCT CONTEXT

Another important shift was happening across Amazon Health while this work was being developed.

Health AI was emerging as a new front door into healthcare.

The original telehealth model assumes a relatively linear path:

Landing page → Condition → Intake → Visit

An AI-led care layer creates a different model.

A customer might begin with a question:

Can I take Zepbound?
Why do I need these labs?
I need to renew my medication.
What is my next step?

Instead of forcing every customer through the same front door, Health AI can potentially understand the question, medication context, health records, or lab information and route the customer directly to the relevant point in care.

That makes the underlying longitudinal architecture even more important.

The durable part of the product is not the landing page.

It is the persistent treatment state underneath it.

Different surfaces can become different front doors into the same Case:

Amazon Search
Amazon Pharmacy
One Medical
Email
Notifications
Health AI

This is why I would not describe Health AI as invalidating the work.

It reinforces the need for the Case.

The original work established the longitudinal care model.

Health AI creates a more flexible way for patients to interact with that model.


20. IF I WERE EXTENDING THE PRODUCT TODAY

I would not redesign the product around a different longitudinal model.

The work already established that foundation.

What I would change today is how the patient interacts with that system.

Health AI creates an opportunity to make the Case more proactive.

Instead of expecting customers to repeatedly interpret the system themselves, the product could answer:

Where am I in treatment?
What medication am I currently on?
What is the next thing I need to do?
Do I need labs before my next renewal?
When is my next clinical action due?
Is my prescription ready?
Do I need another visit?

The AI layer could then help the patient navigate to the appropriate action, while leaving medical decisions with clinicians.

The core product principle remains the same:

Care is not a sequence of screens or visits.

It is a continuously changing patient state.

The interface can evolve around that state.


21. THE MAIN PRODUCT-DESIGN DECISIONS TO EMPHASIZE IN AN INTERVIEW

If this case is turned into a presentation, it should not become a screen-by-screen walkthrough.

The strongest design decisions are:

1. Reframing the problem from “add weight loss to PPV” to “make PPV support longitudinal care.”

2. Mapping the patient journey across many months before designing individual screens.

3. Introducing the Case as a layer above individual visits.

4. Translating clinical and regulatory rules into clear continue, guidance, and stop states.

5. Designing purposeful friction rather than simply minimizing friction.

6. Making cross-company handoffs with Labcorp and Amazon Pharmacy feel like one experience.

7. Using usability testing to validate the overall architecture while identifying specific conversion and trust problems.

8. Reframing pricing from isolated fees to total longitudinal financial commitment.

9. Reframing medication selection from a price comparison to an informed clinical preference.

10. Extending the experience into email and notifications because longitudinal care continues when the customer is outside the product.

11. Designing the system so multiple future entry points—including Health AI—can resume the same underlying care state.

24. THE SIMPLEST INTERVIEW TAKEAWAY

The hardest part of the project was not designing a questionnaire or treatment-plan screen.

It was taking a business model designed for a transaction and asking:

What needs to become true for this product to support a relationship?

That required product architecture, clinical and legal translation, cross-team coordination, user research, lifecycle design, and detailed UX.

The kind of product problem I enjoy most is:

Complex underneath.
Obvious to the customer.
